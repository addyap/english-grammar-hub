
##### another-vs-other-vs-the-other (B1)
 -- Ex1: Another, Other, or The Other?
  1. I don't like this shirt. Can you show me ___ one?
     ANS: another   OPTS: another / other / the other
     WHY: One more/different shirt from an unlimited set of shirts takes another + singular noun.
  2. This coffee is cold. Could I have ___ cup, please?
     ANS: another   OPTS: another / other / others
     WHY: another + singular countable noun for one more from an open set.
  3. I have two pens; one is blue and ___ is red.
     ANS: the other   OPTS: the other / another / other
     WHY: The specific remaining one out of exactly two takes the other + singular noun.
  4. There are five rooms upstairs. Two are bedrooms and ___ rooms are offices.
     ANS: the other   OPTS: the other / another / others
     WHY: The specific remaining items from a known, limited group take the other + plural noun.
  5. I need to hear ___ opinions before I decide anything.
     ANS: other   OPTS: other / another / the other
     WHY: More of the same general kind (plural, open set) takes other, not another.
  6. This restaurant is full. Let's try ___ one down the street.
     ANS: another   OPTS: another / other / the other
     WHY: A different one from an unlimited set of restaurants takes another.
  7. She's not home right now — she's at ___ friend's house.
     ANS: another   OPTS: another / other / the other
     WHY: another + singular noun for one (different) out of many possible friends.
  8. Of the two roads, one leads to the beach and ___ leads to the mountains.
     ANS: the other   OPTS: the other / another / other
     WHY: The specific remaining one out of exactly two takes the other.
  9. We don't have enough chairs — we need ___ chair for the extra guest.
     ANS: another   OPTS: another / other / the other
     WHY: One more, from an unlimited supply of chairs, takes another.
  10. I couldn't find any milk, so I bought some ___ groceries instead.
     ANS: other   OPTS: other / another / the other
     WHY: other + plural noun for more of the same general kind, open set.
 -- Ex2: Others, The Others & Mixed Practice
  1. Some students prefer studying alone; ___ prefer studying in groups.
     ANS: others   OPTS: others / the others / another
     WHY: A general statement about students in general (an open, unlimited set) uses others, not the others.
  2. I invited ten guests. Two couldn't come, but ___ all showed up.
     ANS: the others   OPTS: the others / others / another
     WHY: A pronoun for the specific remaining items from a known group of ten uses the others.
  3. One of my sisters is a doctor; ___ is a teacher.
     ANS: the other   OPTS: the other / other / another
     WHY: The specific remaining one out of exactly two takes the other.
  4. Some tourists like quiet beaches; ___ prefer busy, lively ones.
     ANS: others   OPTS: others / the others / another
     WHY: A pronoun for an open, unlimited set of people uses others.
  5. I tried on three jackets. I liked one, but ___ two didn't fit.
     ANS: the other   OPTS: the other / other / another
     WHY: The specific remaining items from a known group of three take the other + plural noun.
  6. This plan didn't work, so we need to think of ___ solution.
     ANS: another   OPTS: another / other / the other
     WHY: A different one from an unlimited set of possible solutions takes another.
  7. Half the team voted yes, and ___ voted no.
     ANS: the others   OPTS: the others / others / another
     WHY: A pronoun for the specific remaining half of a known, limited team uses the others.
  8. Could you give me ___ information about the course before I apply?
     ANS: other   OPTS: other / another / the other
     WHY: other + uncountable noun for more of the same general kind, open set.
  9. I only found one of my shoes. Have you seen ___ one?
     ANS: the other   OPTS: the other / another / other
     WHY: The specific remaining item out of exactly two (a pair) takes the other.
  10. Some people learn best by reading; ___ learn best by doing.
     ANS: others   OPTS: others / the others / another
     WHY: A pronoun for an open, unlimited group uses others, not the others.

##### future-time-clauses (B1)
 -- Ex1: Time Clause or Future Will?
  1. I'll call you as soon as I ___ (arrive) at the airport.
     ANS: arrive   OPTS: arrive / will arrive / arrived
     WHY: the time clause after as soon as uses a present tense, never will, even though it's about the future.
  2. Before you ___ (leave), please turn off the lights.
     ANS: leave   OPTS: leave / will leave / left
     WHY: before + present tense for a future time clause; will never goes here.
  3. Tonight, we ___ (go) out for dinner when my parents arrive.
     ANS: will go   OPTS: will go / go / went
     WHY: this is the main clause stating the future plan; 'tonight' confirms a specific future event, not a habit.
  4. I won't leave until she ___ (call) me back.
     ANS: calls   OPTS: calls / will call / called
     WHY: until + present tense (3rd person -s) in the time clause; will never goes here.
  5. By the time we ___ (get) to the cinema, the film will have started.
     ANS: get   OPTS: get / will get / got
     WHY: by the time + present tense; the main clause (will have started) carries the future meaning instead.
  6. As soon as the rain ___ (stop), we'll go for a walk.
     ANS: stops   OPTS: stops / will stop / stopped
     WHY: as soon as + present tense in the time clause.
  7. After I ___ (finish) my homework, I'm going to watch a movie.
     ANS: finish   OPTS: finish / will finish / finished
     WHY: after + present tense; 'finished' would wrongly suggest it already happened.
  8. When she ___ (get) here, we can start the meeting.
     ANS: gets   OPTS: gets / will get / got
     WHY: when + present tense (3rd person -s) in the time clause.
  9. I'll have dinner ready by the time you ___ (get) home.
     ANS: get   OPTS: get / will get / got
     WHY: by the time + present tense in the time clause.
  10. She won't be happy until you ___ (apologize).
     ANS: apologize   OPTS: apologize / will apologize / apologized
     WHY: until + present tense in the time clause.
 -- Ex2: Mixed Practice
  1. While you ___ (cook), I'll set the table.
     ANS: cook   OPTS: cook / will cook / cooked
     WHY: while + present tense in the time clause; will never goes here.
  2. We'll start the presentation as soon as everyone ___ (arrive).
     ANS: arrives   OPTS: arrives / will arrive / arrived
     WHY: as soon as + present tense (3rd person -s).
  3. I'm going to wait here until the bus ___ (come).
     ANS: comes   OPTS: comes / will come / came
     WHY: until + present tense in the time clause.
  4. You can borrow my car after I ___ (get) back from the airport.
     ANS: get   OPTS: get / will get / got
     WHY: after + present tense in the time clause.
  5. The kids will be so excited when they ___ (see) the surprise.
     ANS: see   OPTS: see / will see / saw
     WHY: when + present tense in the time clause; the main clause (will be excited) already carries the future meaning.
  6. Before the guests ___ (arrive), we need to clean the house.
     ANS: arrive   OPTS: arrive / will arrive / arrived
     WHY: before + present tense in the time clause.
  7. As soon as I ___ (hear) the news, I'll let you know.
     ANS: hear   OPTS: hear / will hear / heard
     WHY: as soon as + present tense in the time clause.
  8. I won't relax until the exam ___ (be) over.
     ANS: is   OPTS: is / will be / was
     WHY: until + present tense (be → is) in the time clause.
  9. By the time the movie ___ (start), we'll already be in our seats.
     ANS: starts   OPTS: starts / will start / started
     WHY: by the time + present tense (3rd person -s) in the time clause.
  10. When you ___ (need) help, just ask me.
     ANS: need   OPTS: need / will need / needed
     WHY: when + present tense in the time clause, even for a general 'whenever' meaning.

##### make-vs-do (A2)
 -- Ex1: Make or Do?
  1. She ___ (make/do) a big mistake when she signed that contract.
     ANS: made   OPTS: made / did / make
     WHY: made a mistake is a fixed expression; did a mistake is wrong.
  2. Can you ___ (make/do) me a favor?
     ANS: do   OPTS: do / make / doing
     WHY: do someone a favor is a fixed expression; make a favor is wrong.
  3. I need to ___ (make/do) my homework before dinner.
     ANS: do   OPTS: do / make / doing
     WHY: do homework is a fixed expression; make homework is wrong.
  4. They're going to ___ (make/do) a big decision about the company's future.
     ANS: make   OPTS: make / do / making
     WHY: make a decision is a fixed expression; do a decision is wrong.
  5. Please ___ (make/do) your bed before you leave for school.
     ANS: make   OPTS: make / do / making
     WHY: make the bed is one of the fixed exceptions worth memorizing on its own.
  6. She spends an hour every morning ___ (make/do) her hair.
     ANS: doing   OPTS: doing / making / made
     WHY: do your hair is one of the fixed exceptions worth memorizing on its own.
  7. We ___ (make/do) a lot of progress on the project yesterday.
     ANS: made   OPTS: made / did / make
     WHY: make progress is a fixed expression; do progress is wrong.
  8. He never ___ (make/do) his best in exams — he just doesn't try.
     ANS: does   OPTS: does / makes / doing
     WHY: do your best is a fixed expression; make your best is wrong.
  9. I'm going to ___ (make/do) an appointment with the dentist tomorrow.
     ANS: make   OPTS: make / do / making
     WHY: make an appointment is a fixed expression; do an appointment is wrong.
  10. The construction workers ___ (make/do) so much noise every morning.
     ANS: make   OPTS: make / do / making
     WHY: make noise is a fixed expression; do noise is wrong.
 -- Ex2: Mixed Practice
  1. He ___ (make/do) a lot of money from his online business.
     ANS: makes   OPTS: makes / does / doing
     WHY: make money is a fixed expression.
  2. I ___ (make/do) some exercise every morning before work.
     ANS: do   OPTS: do / make / doing
     WHY: do exercise is a fixed expression.
  3. The children ___ (make/do) a huge mess in the kitchen while I was out.
     ANS: made   OPTS: made / did / make
     WHY: make a mess is a fixed expression.
  4. She ___ (make/do) a real effort to learn the language before moving abroad.
     ANS: made   OPTS: made / did / make
     WHY: make an effort is a fixed expression.
  5. We need to ___ (make/do) the cleaning before the guests arrive.
     ANS: do   OPTS: do / make / doing
     WHY: do the cleaning is a fixed expression.
  6. I have nothing to ___ (make/do) this afternoon — want to meet up?
     ANS: do   OPTS: do / make / doing
     WHY: do nothing/something/anything all use do, never make.
  7. He ___ (make/do) very well on his exams last year.
     ANS: did   OPTS: did / made / doing
     WHY: do well/badly (on a test) is a fixed expression; make well is wrong.
  8. They ___ (make/do) friends quickly wherever they travel.
     ANS: make   OPTS: make / do / made
     WHY: make friends is a fixed expression.
  9. Could you ___ (make/do) a phone call to confirm the reservation?
     ANS: make   OPTS: make / do / making
     WHY: make a phone call is a fixed expression.
  10. Our company ___ (make/do) business with clients all over the world.
     ANS: does   OPTS: does / makes / doing
     WHY: do business is a fixed expression.

##### as-if-as-though (B2)
 -- Ex1: As If / As Though: Plausible or Hypothetical?
  1. He doesn't know anything about cars, but he talks as if he ___ (know) everything.
     ANS: knew   OPTS: knew / knows / will know
     WHY: the sentence already says he doesn't know — that falsity requires the hypothetical past, not the present or will.
  2. I can hear something outside — it sounds as if it ___ (rain).
     ANS: is raining   OPTS: is raining / rained / will rain
     WHY: a plausible, maybe-true sensory judgment takes the present tense after as if.
  3. She's actually terrified of flying, but she boards the plane as if she ___ (feel) totally relaxed.
     ANS: felt   OPTS: felt / feels / will feel
     WHY: 'actually terrified' establishes the comparison is false, so the hypothetical past is required.
  4. Something smells as if it ___ (burn) in the kitchen.
     ANS: is burning   OPTS: is burning / burned / had burned
     WHY: a plausible, maybe-true sensory judgment takes the present tense after as if.
  5. He's the newest person on the team, but he talks as if he ___ (be) the most experienced person in the room.
     ANS: were   OPTS: were / is / will be
     WHY: 'the newest person' directly contradicts being 'the most experienced', so the hypothetical past (were) is needed.
  6. The kids are wide awake, but they're behaving as if they ___ (be) exhausted.
     ANS: were   OPTS: were / are / will be
     WHY: 'wide awake' contradicts 'exhausted', so the hypothetical past (were) is needed.
  7. You look as if you ___ (need) a coffee.
     ANS: need   OPTS: need / needed / will need
     WHY: nothing in the sentence marks this as false, so the plausible present tense is correct.
  8. She doesn't even like sports, but she talks as if she ___ (know) every football rule.
     ANS: knew   OPTS: knew / knows / will know
     WHY: 'doesn't even like sports' establishes the comparison is false, requiring the hypothetical past.
  9. It's boiling in here — it feels as if the heating ___ (be) stuck on full blast.
     ANS: is   OPTS: is / was / will be
     WHY: a plausible, maybe-true sensory judgment takes the present tense after as if.
  10. He's broke, but he spends money as if he ___ (have) unlimited funds.
     ANS: had   OPTS: had / has / will have
     WHY: 'he's broke' establishes the comparison is false, requiring the hypothetical past.
 -- Ex2: Mixed Practice
  1. She looked as if she ___ (run) a marathon, though she'd only walked from the car.
     ANS: had run   OPTS: had run / ran / runs
     WHY: 'though she'd only walked' places the false event earlier than the looking, so past perfect is needed.
  2. He seemed confused when he walked in, as if he ___ (never/hear) the news before that moment.
     ANS: had never heard   OPTS: had never heard / never heard / doesn't hear
     WHY: 'before that moment' anchors an earlier point than 'seemed', requiring past perfect.
  3. The room smelled as if someone ___ (cook) there earlier that morning.
     ANS: had cooked   OPTS: had cooked / cooked / was cooking
     WHY: 'earlier that morning' marks a point before the smelling, requiring past perfect.
  4. She's not injured, but she's walking as if she ___ (hurt) herself yesterday.
     ANS: had hurt   OPTS: had hurt / hurt / hurts
     WHY: 'yesterday' places the hypothetical injury before the present walking, requiring past perfect.
  5. That guitar sounds as if it ___ (need) new strings.
     ANS: needs   OPTS: needs / needed / will need
     WHY: a plausible, maybe-true sensory judgment takes the present tense after as if.
  6. He's calm now, but earlier he was shouting as if the house ___ (catch) fire.
     ANS: had caught   OPTS: had caught / caught / catches
     WHY: the hypothetical fire is set before the past shouting, requiring past perfect.
  7. You sound as if you ___ (have) a cold — is your nose blocked?
     ANS: have   OPTS: have / had / will have
     WHY: a plausible, maybe-true sensory judgment takes the present tense after as if.
  8. He wasn't even there, but he described the accident as if he ___ (see) it happen.
     ANS: had seen   OPTS: had seen / saw / sees
     WHY: 'wasn't even there' places the false seeing before the describing, requiring past perfect.
  9. This soup tastes as if it ___ (need) more salt.
     ANS: needs   OPTS: needs / needed / will need
     WHY: a plausible, maybe-true sensory judgment takes the present tense after as if.
  10. She's never even been to Japan, but she talks about it as if she ___ (visit) it many times.
     ANS: had visited   OPTS: had visited / visited / visits
     WHY: 'never even been' establishes the visits as false and earlier than the talking, requiring past perfect.

##### remember-forget-stop-try-ing-vs-to (B2)
 -- Ex1: Remember & Forget: -ing or To?
  1. I clearly remember ___ (lock) the door before we left — I can picture doing it.
     ANS: locking   OPTS: locking / to lock / lock
     WHY: 'I can picture doing it' marks this as a memory of a completed action.
  2. Please remember ___ (buy) milk on your way home — we're completely out.
     ANS: to buy   OPTS: to buy / buying / buy
     WHY: 'we're completely out' marks this as an upcoming duty, not yet done.
  3. I'll never forget ___ (see) the pyramids for the first time — it was unforgettable.
     ANS: seeing   OPTS: seeing / to see / see
     WHY: 'for the first time... unforgettable' marks this as a memory of a past event.
  4. He forgot ___ (send) the report, so his boss never received it.
     ANS: to send   OPTS: to send / sending / send
     WHY: 'never received it' shows the action was never done — a failed obligation.
  5. Do you remember ___ (meet) at that conference two years ago?
     ANS: meeting   OPTS: meeting / to meet / meet
     WHY: 'two years ago' marks this as a memory of something already done.
  6. Don't forget ___ (call) the dentist tomorrow to confirm your appointment.
     ANS: to call   OPTS: to call / calling / call
     WHY: 'tomorrow' marks this as an upcoming, not-yet-done duty.
  7. I remember ___ (turn) off the stove — I checked it right before we left.
     ANS: turning   OPTS: turning / to turn / turn
     WHY: 'I checked it right before we left' marks this as a memory of a completed action.
  8. She forgot ___ (bring) her umbrella, so she got soaked in the rain.
     ANS: to bring   OPTS: to bring / bringing / bring
     WHY: 'got soaked in the rain' shows the action was never done — a failed obligation.
  9. I remember ___ (visit) this town as a child — everything looks so different now.
     ANS: visiting   OPTS: visiting / to visit / visit
     WHY: 'as a child... looks different now' marks this as a memory of the past.
  10. Please remember ___ (turn) off the lights before you go to bed tonight.
     ANS: to turn   OPTS: to turn / turning / turn
     WHY: 'before you go to bed tonight' marks this as an upcoming reminder, not yet done.

  1. He stopped ___ (smoke) five years ago and hasn't touched a cigarette since.
     ANS: smoking   OPTS: smoking / to smoke / smoke

  3. My computer keeps freezing — try ___ (restart) it and see if that fixes it.

  4. I tried ___ (open) the jar for ten minutes, but the lid just wouldn't budge.

     WHY: 'for ten minutes... wouldn't budge' marks this as a genuine, difficult effort.
  5. The doctor told him to stop ___ (eat) sugar completely for the sake of his health.
     ANS: eating   OPTS: eating / to eat / eat
     WHY: 'completely' shows the activity itself is meant to end.
  6. While jogging, she stopped ___ (tie) her shoelace, then kept running.
     ANS: to tie   OPTS: to tie / tying / tie
     WHY: 'then kept running' shows this was a pause for a new purpose, not the end of the jog.
  7. If the printer jams again, try ___ (turn) it off and on.
     ANS: turning   OPTS: turning / to turn / turn
     WHY: trying a method/solution to see if it works takes -ing after try.
  8. He tried ___ (lift) the sofa on his own, but it was far too heavy for one person.

  9. I remember ___ (leave) my keys on the kitchen table — I can still picture them there.

##### ability-permission-modals (B1)
 -- Ex1: Ability: General Past vs One-Time Success
  1. When I was a child, I ___ (can/climb) trees easily.
     ANS: could climb   OPTS: could climb / was able to climb / managed to climb
     WHY: General/repeated past ability, signalled by 'when I was a child' — could.
  2. Despite the flat tyre, she ___ (be able to/finish) the marathon.
     ANS: was able to finish   OPTS: was able to finish / could finish / can finish
     WHY: A single specific achievement despite difficulty — was able to, not could.
  3. She ___ (can/speak) three languages by the time she was ten.
     ANS: could speak   OPTS: could speak / was able to speak / managed to speak
     WHY: A skill built up over time (repeated/general ability) — could.
  4. After three attempts, he finally ___ (manage to/open) the jar.
     ANS: managed to open   OPTS: managed to open / could open / can open
     WHY: One specific successful event, marked by 'finally' and 'after three attempts' — managed to.
  5. In those days, we ___ (can/leave) our doors unlocked.
     ANS: could leave   OPTS: could leave / were able to leave / managed to leave
     WHY: A general, habitual past situation ('in those days') — could.
  6. I ___ (not/can/find) my keys yesterday.
     ANS: couldn't find   OPTS: couldn't find / wasn't able to find / didn't manage to find
     WHY: A one-time past failure/negative — could works fine here; the could-restriction only applies to one-time positive achievements.
  7. Somehow, the team ___ (manage to/win) the match despite being down 2-0.
     ANS: managed to win   OPTS: managed to win / could win / can win
     WHY: One specific event with an effort/difficulty marker ('somehow', 'despite') — managed to.
  8. Once I finish this course, I ___ (will be able to/teach) English professionally.
     ANS: will be able to teach   OPTS: will be able to teach / could teach / can teach
     WHY: Future ability — will be able to.
  9. Every summer at my grandparents' house, I ___ (can/swim) in the lake all day.
     ANS: could swim   OPTS: could swim / was able to swim / managed to swim
     WHY: A repeated, habitual past ability ('every summer') — could.
  10. Yesterday's exam was hard, but she ___ (be able to/pass) it.
     ANS: was able to pass   OPTS: was able to pass / could pass / can pass
     WHY: One specific past success (a single exam) — was able to, not could.
 -- Ex2: Permission: Can, Could & May
  1. ___ I borrow a pen from you? (asking a close friend)
     ANS: Can   OPTS: Can / May / Must
     WHY: Informal permission between friends — can fits naturally; may would sound stiff here.
  2. ___ I leave early today? (asking your manager, fairly politely)
     ANS: Could   OPTS: Could / Can't / Should
     WHY: A more polite, slightly formal request for permission to a manager — could.
  3. In a formal letter to your professor: ___ I request an extension on the deadline?
     ANS: May   OPTS: May / Can / Could
     WHY: The most formal, polite way to ask for permission in writing — may.
  4. The teacher said, "You ___ leave now, class is over."
     ANS: may   OPTS: may / must / should
     WHY: may is also used to formally grant permission.
  5. ___ I come in? — Yes, you may. (formal office setting)
     ANS: May   OPTS: May / Must / Should
     WHY: May I...? is the classic formal request for permission, and 'you may' formally grants it.
  6. ___ I use your phone for a second? (to a close friend)
     ANS: Can   OPTS: Can / May / Must
     WHY: Everyday informal permission request between friends — can, not the stiffer may.
  7. ___ we open the window? It's quite warm in here. (polite, to colleagues)
     ANS: Could   OPTS: Could / Must / Should
     WHY: A polite request for permission, more formal than can but less formal than may.
  8. You ___ sit anywhere you like — there's no assigned seating.
     ANS: can   OPTS: can / must / should
     WHY: Informal permission being granted — can.
  9. The engraved brass plaque at the entrance reads: 'Visitors ___ not touch or photograph the exhibits without prior written permission.'
     ANS: may   OPTS: may / can / should
     WHY: Formal written permission rule (prohibition) — the legalistic phrase 'without prior written permission' anchors the formal register of 'may not'; 'can not' would clash with that register.
  10. ___ I ask you a personal question? (polite, slightly formal, to someone you don't know well)
     ANS: May   OPTS: May / Must / Should
     WHY: A polite, formal way to ask permission before a sensitive question — may.

##### academic-hedging (C2)
 -- Ex1: Hedging Structures
  1. It ___ that the strategy failed to achieve its goals.
     ANS: could be argued   OPTS: could be argued / could argue / could be argued for
     WHY: impersonal passive it could be argued that... — could argue lacks the passive, and could be argued for cannot be followed directly by that.
  2. It ___ that early intervention improves outcomes.
     ANS: has been suggested   OPTS: has been suggested / has suggested / has been suggesting
     WHY: impersonal passive it has been suggested that... — has suggested needs a specific agent, and has been suggesting (progressive passive) is not the fixed hedging form.
  3. There ___ a tendency for prices to rise in summer.
     ANS: is   OPTS: is / are / have
     WHY: there is a tendency agrees with the singular noun tendency, regardless of what follows for.
  4. There appears to be a tendency ___ smaller firms to grow faster.
     ANS: for   OPTS: for / of / that
     WHY: the fixed pattern is tendency for + noun/pronoun + to-infinitive, not tendency of or tendency that.
  5. It ___ that the results support the theory.
     ANS: is widely believed   OPTS: is widely believed / widely believes / is widely believing
     WHY: impersonal passive it is widely believed that... — widely believes needs an agent, and is widely believing is ungrammatical.
  6. This would ___ to support the theory.
     ANS: appear   OPTS: appear / appears / appearing
     WHY: modal + base form: would appear to, not would appears to or would appearing to.
  7. The results seem ___ a correlation between the variables.
     ANS: to suggest   OPTS: to suggest / suggest / suggesting
     WHY: seem is followed by a to-infinitive (seem to suggest), not a bare infinitive or a gerund.
  8. ___, the sample size was too small to draw firm conclusions.
     ANS: Arguably   OPTS: Arguably / Argue / Argument
     WHY: a sentence-initial tentative adverb (arguably) is needed here, not a verb or a noun.
  9. There ___ to be a link between diet and concentration levels.
     ANS: appears   OPTS: appears / appear / appearing
     WHY: there appears to be agrees with the singular existential there and uses the correct verb form, not the plural appear or the non-finite appearing.
  10. It ___ that the reform will reduce costs in the long term.
     ANS: could be argued   OPTS: could be argued / could be argue / could argued
     WHY: the fixed passive form is could be argued — could be argue and could argued are both ungrammatical.
 -- Ex2: Hedging vs Absolute Claims
  1. It is ___ that the theory is flawed — the evidence is far from conclusive.
     ANS: arguable   OPTS: arguable / definitely arguable / undoubtedly arguable

##### result-connectors (B2)
 -- Ex1: Therefore, Consequently & As a Result
  1. The evidence was inconclusive. ___, the case was dismissed.
     ANS: Therefore   OPTS: Therefore / So / As a result of
     WHY: Therefore starts a new sentence after a full stop and takes a comma right after it — so only appears mid-sentence after a comma, and as a result of needs a noun, not a clause.
  2. It rained, ___ we stayed home.
     ANS: so   OPTS: so / therefore / consequently
     WHY: so joins two clauses in one sentence with just a comma before it — therefore/consequently need a full stop or semicolon before them, not just a comma.
  3. The flight was delayed by six hours; ___, many passengers missed their connections.
     ANS: consequently   OPTS: consequently / so / as a result of
     WHY: consequently follows a semicolon and takes its own comma — so can't follow a semicolon like this, and as a result of would need a noun after it, not a full clause.
  4. ___ the delay, we missed our connection.
     ANS: As a result of   OPTS: As a result of / As a result / Therefore
     WHY: As a result of is a preposition followed directly by a noun (the delay) — As a result and Therefore both need a full clause after their comma, not a bare noun phrase before it like this.
  5. The company cut costs. ___, profits improved.
     ANS: As a result   OPTS: As a result / As a result of / So
     WHY: As a result (no of) is a connector starting a new sentence, just like Therefore — As a result of needs a noun/gerund directly after it, and So can't start a new sentence like this.
  6. It rained; ___, we stayed home.
     ANS: therefore   OPTS: therefore / so / as a result of
     WHY: therefore fits correctly after a semicolon — so can't follow a semicolon like this, and as a result of would need a noun after it, not a full clause.
  7. ___ cutting costs, profits improved.
     ANS: As a result of   OPTS: As a result of / As a result / Therefore
     WHY: As a result of is followed directly by a gerund (cutting costs) — As a result and Therefore both need a comma and then a full clause, not a gerund phrase like this.
  8. The report found no evidence of fraud; ___, the investigation was closed.
     ANS: thus   OPTS: thus / so / as a result of
     WHY: thus follows a semicolon and takes its own comma, introducing a summarizing conclusion — so can't follow a semicolon like this, and as a result of needs a noun, not a clause.
  9. She studied all night. ___, she passed the exam easily.
     ANS: Consequently   OPTS: Consequently / As a result of / So
     WHY: Consequently starts a new sentence after a full stop and takes a comma after it — As a result of needs a noun/gerund directly after it, and So can't start a new sentence like this.
  10. It was raining, ___ we stayed home.
     ANS: so   OPTS: so / therefore / hence
     WHY: so is the only one of these that can follow a simple comma mid-sentence — therefore and hence both need a full stop or semicolon before them.
 -- Ex2: Punctuation & Preposition vs Connector
  1. It rained. ___, we stayed home.
     ANS: Therefore   OPTS: Therefore / As a result of / So
     WHY: Therefore correctly starts a new sentence after a full stop and takes a comma after it — As a result of needs a noun/gerund, not a full clause, and So can't start a new sentence like this.
  2. The bridge was closed; ___, traffic was diverted.
     ANS: as a result   OPTS: as a result / as a result of / so

##### non-finite-verbs-overview (B1)
 -- Ex1: Identifying Gerunds, Infinitives & Participles
  1. In 'Swimming is my favourite sport,' the word 'swimming' is a(n) ___.
     ANS: gerund   OPTS: gerund / present participle / infinitive
     WHY: It is the subject of the sentence, a role only a noun-like gerund can fill; it isn't spelled 'to swimming' (no infinitive), and a present participle would need to modify a noun or open a clause, not act as the subject itself.
  2. In 'The swimming pool is closed,' the word 'swimming' is a(n) ___.
     ANS: present participle   OPTS: present participle / gerund / infinitive
     WHY: It modifies the noun 'pool' like an adjective (a pool for swimming), which is the job of a present participle; a gerund would need to function as a noun itself, not describe one.
  3. In 'Swimming across the lake, she felt free,' the word 'swimming' opens a(n) ___ clause.
     ANS: participle   OPTS: participle / noun / adverb
     WHY: The -ing form has no subject of its own before the comma, which rules out a full adverb clause (that needs its own subject and a conjunction like 'while'); it isn't a noun clause either, since it doesn't function as a subject or object — it's a reduced participle clause.
  4. In 'I want to swim,' the phrase 'to swim' is a(n) ___.
     ANS: infinitive   OPTS: infinitive / gerund / present participle
     WHY: 'to' plus the base verb is the infinitive form; it isn't spelled with -ing, which rules out both the gerund and the present participle.
  5. In 'I saw him swim across the lake,' the word 'swim' is a(n) ___.
     ANS: bare infinitive   OPTS: bare infinitive / gerund / past participle
     WHY: After a perception verb plus an object (saw him), English uses the infinitive without 'to' — it isn't -ing (so not a gerund), and it isn't the irregular past participle form 'swum'.
  6. In 'The broken vase lay on the floor,' the word 'broken' is a(n) ___.
     ANS: past participle   OPTS: past participle / present participle / gerund
     WHY: 'broken' is the irregular past participle used as an adjective; it isn't spelled with -ing, which rules out the present participle and the gerund.
  7. In 'I enjoy swimming in the sea,' the word 'swimming' is the ___ of the verb 'enjoy'.
     ANS: gerund object   OPTS: gerund object / infinitive object / present participle
     WHY: enjoy is always followed by a gerund object, not an infinitive; and here 'swimming' is the object of 'enjoy', not an adjective describing a noun, which rules out the present participle.
  8. In 'He left without saying goodbye,' the word 'saying' is a(n) ___ after the preposition 'without'.
     ANS: gerund   OPTS: gerund / infinitive / present participle
     WHY: As the object of the preposition 'without', 'saying' functions as a noun (a gerund), not an adjective — which rules out the present participle — and prepositions are never followed by a to-infinitive.
  9. In 'Her singing improved a lot this year,' the word 'singing' is a(n) ___ modified by the possessive 'her'.
     ANS: gerund   OPTS: gerund / present participle / infinitive
     WHY: Only a gerund behaves like a noun and can be modified by a possessive such as 'her'; a present participle is adjectival and never takes a possessive.
  10. In 'Feeling tired, she went to bed early,' the phrase 'Feeling tired' opens a(n) ___ clause.
     ANS: participle   OPTS: participle / independent / noun
     WHY: 'Feeling tired' has no subject of its own before 'she went to bed' and cannot stand alone, which rules out an independent clause; it isn't functioning as a subject or object either, which rules out a noun clause.
 -- Ex2: Choosing the Right Non-Finite Form
  1. I admit ___ (make) a mistake.
     ANS: making   OPTS: making / to make / make
     WHY: admit is always followed by a gerund, never a to-infinitive or bare infinitive.
  2. They refused ___ (pay) the fine.
     ANS: to pay   OPTS: to pay / paying / pay
     WHY: refuse is always followed by a to-infinitive, never a gerund or bare infinitive.
  3. I heard her ___ (sing) the whole song.
     ANS: sing   OPTS: sing / to sing / sings
     WHY: After a perception verb plus an object (heard her), English uses the bare infinitive without to; 'sings' is a finite form and can't follow an object like this.
  4. The teacher let us ___ (leave) early.
     ANS: leave   OPTS: leave / to leave / leaving
     WHY: let + object is always followed by the bare infinitive, never to or -ing.
  5. I'm looking forward to ___ (see) you.
     ANS: seeing   OPTS: seeing / see / to see
     WHY: Prepositions take a gerund, even when the preposition is the word to itself — look forward to seeing, not to see.
  6. She's used to ___ (get up) early.
     ANS: getting up   OPTS: getting up / get up / to get up
     WHY: be used to is preposition to + gerund, meaning accustomed to a habit; the 's (is) rules out the unrelated pattern used to + base verb, which describes a past habit and needs no is.
  7. The film was really ___ (bore).
     ANS: boring   OPTS: boring / bored / bore
     WHY: The film causes the feeling, so the present participle boring is used as the adjective; bored would describe a person who feels bored, and bore is not an adjective form.
  8. I was ___ (bore) during the meeting.
     ANS: bored   OPTS: bored / boring / bore
     WHY: The person experiences the feeling, so the past participle bored is used; boring would describe something that causes boredom, and bore is not an adjective form.
  9. There's no point ___ (argue) about it.
     ANS: arguing   OPTS: arguing / to argue / argue
     WHY: The fixed expression there's no point is always followed by a gerund, not a to-infinitive or bare infinitive.
  10. ___ (Live) alone, she learned to enjoy her own company.
     ANS: Living   OPTS: Living / Lived / To live
     WHY: A participle clause describing an ongoing state uses the present participle Living (= Because she lived alone); the past participle Lived doesn't fit an intransitive verb like live used this way, and To live would signal purpose, not reason.

##### numbers-fractions-percentages (B1)
 -- Ex1: Fractions & Percentages
  1. 2/3 of the students passed the exam. In words: ___ of the students passed the exam.
     ANS: Two thirds   OPTS: Two thirds / Two third / Two-thirds's
     WHY: The numerator (two) is more than one, so the ordinal denominator must be plural: two thirds, not two third.
  2. 3/4 of the pizza was eaten. In words: ___ was eaten.
     ANS: Three quarters   OPTS: Three quarters / Three quarter / A three quarters
     WHY: With a numerator greater than one, the denominator takes -s: three quarters. Adding "a" before a plural cardinal + ordinal combination is wrong.
  3. 1/4 of a litre remains. In words: ___ of a litre remains.
     ANS: A quarter   OPTS: A quarter / Quarter / The quarter
     WHY: A fraction with numerator one used as a noun needs the indefinite article: a quarter — not bare "quarter", and not "the quarter", which would wrongly suggest one specific, already-known quarter.
  4. I bought ___ kilo of cheese. (1/2)
     ANS: half a   OPTS: half a / a half of / a half
     WHY: half combines directly with a noun using half + a + noun (half a kilo), with no "of" before the bare indefinite noun phrase.
  5. The film lasted ___ hours. (1 1/2)
     ANS: one and a half   OPTS: one and a half / one and half / a one and half
     WHY: Mixed numbers use cardinal + "and" + the fraction, and the fraction still needs its own article: one and a half, never one and half without "a".
  6. 25 ___ of students walk to school.
     ANS: percent   OPTS: percent / percents / percentage
     WHY: percent is invariable and follows a number directly; it never takes -s, and percentage (the general noun for the concept) isn't used directly after a number like this.
  7. In British English, 50% is often written as 50 ___.
     ANS: per cent   OPTS: per cent / percent / procent
     WHY: British English commonly splits it into two words, per cent, while American English writes it as one word, percent — the sentence specifies British English.
  8. Sixty percent ___ the population owns a car.
     ANS: of   OPTS: of / from / percent of
     WHY: Percent + of + the group is the standard structure; "percent" is already given once in the sentence, so repeating it in the gap would be redundant and ungrammatical.
  9. The meeting lasted ___ of an hour. (3/4)
     ANS: three quarters   OPTS: three quarters / three quarter / the three quarters
     WHY: The numerator three (more than one) requires the plural denominator quarters; general fraction expressions of time don't take "the".
  10. ___ my friends live abroad. (1/2)
     ANS: Half of   OPTS: Half of / Half / A half of
     WHY: Before a possessive determiner + noun (my friends), half needs "of": half of my friends. Only before a bare indefinite noun does half attach directly without "of" (half a kilo).
 -- Ex2: Large Numbers & Phone Numbers
  1. There were exactly 300 people at the concert. In words: There were ___ people at the concert.
     ANS: three hundred   OPTS: three hundred / three hundreds / three hundred of
     WHY: Right after a specific number ("exactly 300"), hundred stays singular with no -s and no of: three hundred people.
  2. ___ of people attended the festival, though nobody counted exactly. (hundreds)
     ANS: Hundreds   OPTS: Hundreds / Hundred / Three hundreds
     WHY: "Though nobody counted exactly" signals a vague quantity, which needs the plural + of form: hundreds of people, not the singular hundred or a specific number combined with -s.
  3. She has sold ___ of copies of her book since it came out. (thousands)

     WHY: Followed by "of copies", the vague-quantity plural form is required: thousands of copies, not the singular thousand or the ungrammatical a thousands.
  4. The painting sold for exactly ___ dollars. (2,000,000)
     ANS: two million   OPTS: two million / two millions / two million of
     WHY: "Exactly" signals a specific number, so million stays singular with no -s and no of directly before the noun: two million dollars.
  5. 254, spoken in British style: In words, that's ___.
     ANS: two hundred and fifty-four   OPTS: two hundred and fifty-four / two hundred fifty-four / two fifty four hundred
     WHY: British English usually adds "and" before the tens/units in a compound number, as the sentence specifies; the American form drops it, and the third option puts the words in an impossible order.
  6. 254, spoken in American style: In words, that's ___.

     WHY: American English often drops "and" before the tens/units, as the sentence specifies; the British form keeps it, and the third option is not a real way to say the number.

  8. In British English phone numbers, 0 is usually read as ___, not as the word zero.
     ANS: oh   OPTS: oh / zero / ten
     WHY: "Not as the word zero" explicitly rules out zero itself; British English commonly uses oh for 0 in phone numbers instead, and ten has nothing to do with the digit 0.
  9. In British English, when a phone number has two identical digits in a row, like 33, it is often read as ___ three, not three three.
     ANS: double   OPTS: double / two / second
     WHY: "Not three three" rules out simply repeating the digit; double three is the idiom for a repeated digit, while two three or second three would mean something different.
  10. Nearly ___ of a million people live in the city. (3/4)
     ANS: three quarters   OPTS: three quarters / three quarter / three-quarters's
     WHY: The numerator three (more than one) requires the plural denominator quarters, combined here with a large number: three quarters of a million.

##### parts-of-speech-overview (A1)
 -- Ex1: Identify the Part of Speech
  1. The teacher smiled. "Teacher" is a/an ___.
     ANS: noun   OPTS: noun / verb / adjective
     WHY: "Teacher" names a person (the subject of the sentence) — a noun; "smiled" is already the verb, and there is no noun here for "teacher" to describe, so it can't be an adjective.
  2. The teacher smiled. "Smiled" is a/an ___.
     ANS: verb   OPTS: verb / noun / adjective
     WHY: "Smiled" shows the action the subject (teacher) does — a verb; "teacher" already fills the noun/subject slot, and "smiled" doesn't describe a noun, so it isn't an adjective.
  3. She has a happy dog. "Happy" is a/an ___.
     ANS: adjective   OPTS: adjective / adverb / noun
     WHY: "Happy" sits between the article "a" and the noun "dog", describing it — an adjective. It can't be an adverb because it modifies a noun, not a verb, and "dog" already fills the noun slot.
  4. She smiled happily. "Happily" is a/an ___.
     ANS: adverb   OPTS: adverb / adjective / noun
     WHY: "Happily" describes the verb "smiled" (how she smiled) — an adverb. It can't be an adjective because there is no noun after it to describe.
  5. Maria is tired, so she is going home. "She" is a/an ___.
     ANS: pronoun   OPTS: pronoun / noun / adjective
     WHY: "She" replaces the noun "Maria" instead of repeating it — a pronoun.
  6. The keys are on the table. "On" is a/an ___.
     ANS: preposition   OPTS: preposition / verb / conjunction
     WHY: "On" shows where the keys are in relation to the table — a preposition. "Are" is already the verb, and "on" doesn't join two clauses, so it isn't a conjunction.
  7. I like tea and coffee. "And" is a/an ___.
     ANS: conjunction   OPTS: conjunction / preposition / pronoun
     WHY: "And" joins two nouns, "tea" and "coffee" — a conjunction. It shows no place or time relationship, so it isn't a preposition, and it doesn't replace a noun, so it isn't a pronoun.
  8. Wow! That is a beautiful painting. "Wow" is a/an ___.
     ANS: interjection   OPTS: interjection / adjective / noun
     WHY: "Wow" stands alone and expresses a reaction — an interjection. It doesn't describe the noun "painting", so it isn't an adjective, and it doesn't name anything, so it isn't a noun.
  9. Happiness is important. "Happiness" is a/an ___.
     ANS: noun   OPTS: noun / adjective / verb
     WHY: "Happiness" is the subject of the sentence, naming an idea — a noun. "Is" is already the verb, and "important" already fills the adjective slot.
  10. The soup is hot. "Is" is a/an ___.
     ANS: verb   OPTS: verb / adjective / noun
     WHY: "Is" links the subject "soup" to the description "hot" — a verb (a form of be). "Hot" already fills the adjective slot, and "soup" already fills the noun slot.
 -- Ex2: Same Word, Different Job & Adjective vs Adverb
  1. I run every day. "Run" is a/an ___.
     ANS: verb   OPTS: verb / noun / adjective
     WHY: "Run" follows the subject "I" and shows a repeated action — a verb. There is no article before it, so it isn't a noun here.
  2. I went for a run. "Run" is a/an ___.
     ANS: noun   OPTS: noun / verb / adjective
     WHY: "Run" follows the article "a" — a thing you go for, so a noun. "Went" is already the sentence's verb.
  3. Please light the candle. "Light" is a/an ___.
     ANS: verb   OPTS: verb / noun / adjective
     WHY: "Light" takes a direct object, "the candle" — a verb meaning to set something burning. There is no article before it, so it isn't a noun.
  4. This bag is very light. "Light" is a/an ___.
     ANS: adjective   OPTS: adjective / verb / noun
     WHY: "Light" follows "is very" and describes the bag's weight — an adjective; "very" only modifies adjectives and adverbs, and "is" is already the sentence's verb.
  5. They watch the news every evening. "Watch" is a/an ___.
     ANS: verb   OPTS: verb / noun / adjective
     WHY: "Watch" follows the subject "they" and takes an object, "the news" — a verb. There is no article before it, so it isn't a noun.
  6. She wears a watch on her wrist. "Watch" is a/an ___.
     ANS: noun   OPTS: noun / verb / adjective
     WHY: "Watch" follows the article "a" — a thing worn on the wrist, so a noun. "Wears" is already the sentence's verb.
  7. She sings ___. (beautiful / beautifully)
     ANS: beautifully   OPTS: beautifully / beautiful / beauty
     WHY: "Sings" is a verb, and verbs are described by adverbs, not adjectives — "beautifully", not "beautiful" (an adjective) or "beauty" (a noun).
  8. He drives ___. (careful / carefully)
     ANS: carefully   OPTS: carefully / careful / care
     WHY: "Drives" is a verb, so it needs the adverb "carefully", not the adjective "careful" or the noun "care".
  9. This is a ___ car. (quick / quickly)
     ANS: quick   OPTS: quick / quickly / quickness
     WHY: "Car" is a noun, and nouns are described by adjectives, not adverbs — "a quick car", not "quickly" (an adverb) or "quickness" (a noun).
  10. She is a friendly neighbour. "Friendly" is a/an ___.
     ANS: adjective   OPTS: adjective / adverb / noun
     WHY: "Friendly" sits between the article "a" and the noun "neighbour", describing it — an adjective, even though it ends in -ly like many adverbs; "neighbour" already fills the noun slot, so "friendly" can't be that either.

##### sentence-structure-clauses (B1)
 -- Ex1: Clause Types & Sentence Types
  1. In 'She smiled because she was happy,' the part 'because she was happy' is a(n) ___ clause.
     ANS: dependent   OPTS: dependent / independent / noun
     WHY: It has a subject and verb but starts with because, so it cannot stand alone — it is dependent (specifically an adverb clause here).
  2. Classify: 'The rain stopped.' This is a(n) ___ sentence.
     ANS: simple   OPTS: simple / compound / complex
     WHY: One independent clause and nothing else — a simple sentence.
  3. Classify: 'The rain stopped, and the sun came out.' This is a(n) ___ sentence.
     ANS: compound   OPTS: compound / simple / complex
     WHY: Two independent clauses (each could stand alone) joined by the coordinating conjunction and — a compound sentence.
  4. Classify: 'The rain stopped after the storm passed.' This is a(n) ___ sentence.
     ANS: complex   OPTS: complex / compound / simple
     WHY: One independent clause (the rain stopped) plus one dependent clause (after the storm passed) — a complex sentence.
  5. Classify: 'The rain stopped after the storm passed, and the sun came out.' This is a(n) ___ sentence.
     ANS: compound-complex   OPTS: compound-complex / compound / complex
     WHY: Two independent clauses (the rain stopped / the sun came out) joined by and, plus one dependent clause (after the storm passed) — compound-complex.
  6. In 'the man who called is my uncle,' the clause 'who called' functions as a(n) ___ clause.
     ANS: adjective   OPTS: adjective / noun / adverb
     WHY: It describes the noun 'man', so it is an adjective (relative) clause.
  7. In 'I know that she left,' the clause 'that she left' functions as a(n) ___ clause.
     ANS: noun   OPTS: noun / adjective / adverb
     WHY: It acts as the object of 'know', like a noun would (compare: I know the answer).
  8. Classify: 'Because it was raining, we stayed inside.' This is a(n) ___ sentence.
     ANS: complex   OPTS: complex / compound / simple
     WHY: One dependent clause (because it was raining) plus one independent clause (we stayed inside) — complex, even though the dependent clause comes first.
  9. Classify: 'The rain stopped; the sun came out.' This is a(n) ___ sentence.
     ANS: compound   OPTS: compound / simple / complex
     WHY: Two independent clauses joined by a semicolon (no conjunction needed) — still a compound sentence.
  10. In 'the man standing there,' the phrase 'standing there' is a reduced form of the clause ___.
     ANS: who is standing there   OPTS: who is standing there / who stands there is / that stood there was
     WHY: A reduced clause drops the relative pronoun and be: who is standing there → standing there.
 -- Ex2: Fixing Comma Splices, Fragments & Run-Ons
  1. Fix the comma splice: 'The rain stopped, the sun came out.' → 'The rain stopped, ___ the sun came out.'
     ANS: and   OPTS: and / the / so was
     WHY: A comma alone cannot join two independent clauses; adding the coordinating conjunction and fixes the comma splice.
  2. Identify the error in: 'Because she was tired. She went home.' This is a(n) ___.
     ANS: sentence fragment   OPTS: sentence fragment / comma splice / run-on sentence
     WHY: 'Because she was tired' has a subject and verb but starts with because, so punctuating it as a full sentence with a period makes it an incomplete fragment.
  3. Identify the error in: 'The rain stopped the sun came out.' This is a(n) ___.
     ANS: run-on sentence   OPTS: run-on sentence / sentence fragment / correct as written
     WHY: Two independent clauses are joined with no punctuation and no conjunction at all — a run-on sentence.
  4. Fix the fragment: 'Because she was tired. She went home.' → 'She went home ___ she was tired.'
     ANS: because   OPTS: because / so / and
     WHY: Joining the dependent clause to the independent clause with because fixes the fragment; no period is needed between them.
  5. Identify the error in: 'I like coffee, she prefers tea.' This is a(n) ___.
     ANS: comma splice   OPTS: comma splice / sentence fragment / run-on sentence
     WHY: Two independent clauses (I like coffee / she prefers tea) are joined by only a comma, with no conjunction — a comma splice.
  6. Fix the run-on: 'The rain stopped the sun came out.' → 'The rain stopped___ the sun came out.'
     ANS: ;    OPTS: ;  / , so / . because
     WHY: A semicolon correctly joins two independent clauses with no conjunction needed; a comma alone (without so, and, etc.) would still be a splice.
  7. Identify the error in: 'Although he studied hard. He failed the test.' This is a(n) ___.
     ANS: sentence fragment   OPTS: sentence fragment / comma splice / run-on sentence
     WHY: 'Although he studied hard' is a dependent clause punctuated as if it were complete — a fragment, not a full sentence.
  8. Fix the comma splice correctly: 'She was tired, she went to bed early.' → 'She was tired, ___ she went to bed early.'
     ANS: so   OPTS: so / she / tired
     WHY: Adding the coordinating conjunction so after the comma turns the comma splice into a correct compound sentence.
  9. Choose the correctly punctuated compound sentence: ___
     ANS: It was cold, but we went out anyway.   OPTS: It was cold, but we went out anyway. / It was cold, we went out anyway. / It was cold we went out anyway.
     WHY: A comma plus the coordinating conjunction but correctly joins two independent clauses; the other options are a comma splice and a run-on.
  10. Choose the correctly punctuated complex sentence: ___
     ANS: Although it was cold, we went out.   OPTS: Although it was cold, we went out. / Although it was cold. We went out. / It was cold, although we went out.
     WHY: The dependent clause (although it was cold) comes first and is followed by a comma, then the independent clause — the standard complex-sentence pattern; the second option wrongly punctuates the dependent clause as its own sentence.

##### spelling-rules-suffixes (A2)
 -- Ex1: Doubling & Silent -e
  1. He is ___ (run) to catch the bus.
     ANS: running   OPTS: running / runing / runnning
     WHY: run has a single stressed vowel + consonant, so the n doubles before -ing: run → running.
  2. She ___ (stop) suddenly in the middle of the road.
     ANS: stopped   OPTS: stopped / stoped / stopeed
     WHY: stop doubles the p before -ed: stop → stopped.
  3. He is ___ (visit) his grandmother this week.
     ANS: visiting   OPTS: visiting / visitting / visitteing
     WHY: visit is stressed on the first syllable, so the t does not double: visit → visiting.
  4. They ___ (prefer) tea to coffee.
     ANS: preferred   OPTS: preferred / prefered / preferrred
     WHY: prefer is stressed on the final syllable, so the r doubles before -ed: prefer → preferred.
  5. I'm ___ (make) a cake for the party.
     ANS: making   OPTS: making / makeing / makking
     WHY: make drops the silent -e before a vowel suffix: make → making.
  6. Her smile gave everyone a ___ (hope) feeling.
     ANS: hopeful   OPTS: hopeful / hopful / hopeing
     WHY: Before a consonant suffix (-ful), the silent -e stays: hope → hopeful.
  7. The scratch on the table was barely ___ (notice).
     ANS: noticeable   OPTS: noticeable / noticable / noticeible
     WHY: The -e stays after the soft c so the c keeps its /s/ sound: notice → noticeable.
  8. She ___ (carry) the boxes upstairs by herself.
     ANS: carried   OPTS: carried / carryed / carrys
     WHY: Consonant + y changes to i before -ed: carry → carried.
  9. He is ___ (carry) a heavy bag.
     ANS: carrying   OPTS: carrying / carring / carriing
     WHY: y never changes before -ing, even after a consonant: carry → carrying.
  10. She ___ (watch) the news every evening.
     ANS: watches   OPTS: watches / watchs / watchies
     WHY: watch ends in the sibilant -ch, so add -es instead of -s: watch → watches.
 -- Ex2: y, -es & Mixed Practice
  1. This bag is ___ (big) than that one.
     ANS: bigger   OPTS: bigger / biger / biggest
     WHY: big has a single stressed vowel + consonant, so the g doubles before -er: big → bigger.
  2. I am ___ (help) my sister with her homework.
     ANS: helping   OPTS: helping / helpping / helpeing
     WHY: help ends in two consonants, so nothing doubles: help → helping.
  3. We are ___ (hope) for good weather tomorrow.
     ANS: hoping   OPTS: hoping / hopeing / hopping
     WHY: hope drops the silent -e before -ing: hope → hoping (hopping, with two p's, means jumping, from hop).
  4. Climbing the mountain alone was a ___ (courage) thing to do.
     ANS: courageous   OPTS: courageous / couragous / courageious
     WHY: The -e stays after the soft g so the g keeps its /dʒ/ sound: courage → courageous.
  5. He ___ (play) football every weekend.
     ANS: plays   OPTS: plays / playes / plaies
     WHY: A vowel + y just adds -s, with no spelling change: play → plays.
  6. She is the ___ (happy) person I know.
     ANS: happiest   OPTS: happiest / happyest / happiess
     WHY: Consonant + y changes to i before -est: happy → happiest.
  7. There are two ___ (box) on the table.
     ANS: boxes   OPTS: boxes / boxs / boxies
     WHY: box ends in the sibilant -x, so add -es instead of -s: box → boxes.
  8. The nurse looked after six ___ (baby) in the ward.
     ANS: babies   OPTS: babies / babys / babyes
     WHY: Consonant + y changes to -ies: baby → babies.
  9. In British English, the past tense of travel is ___ (travel).
     ANS: travelled   OPTS: travelled / traveled / travellled
     WHY: British English doubles a final -l even though the stress isn't on the last syllable: travel → travelled (American English uses traveled).
  10. The judges ___ (refer) the case to a higher court.
     ANS: referred   OPTS: referred / refered / referrred
     WHY: refer is stressed on the final syllable, so the r doubles before -ed: refer → referred.
